\begin{abstract}
Mixed-model repeated measures (MMRM) is the default primary
analysis for longitudinal continuous endpoints in clinical
trials. However, the two-stage summary-measures approach, that
is, a per-subject slope or change score followed by ANCOVA,
remains attractive where covariance specification,
degrees-of-freedom choice, and interpretability are practical
concerns. We synthesize three decades of summary-measures
literature and report a Monte Carlo simulation, under a
random-intercept-and-slope generating model, comparing
slope-summary ANCOVA, change-score ANCOVA, and MMRM on bias,
coverage, power, and Type I error. Each method is scored
against its own estimand, with Monte Carlo standard errors
(Morris et al., 2019). With complete data, the slope summary
matched MMRM closely. With intermittent missingness it lost some
power. Under monotone dropout, it remained nearly unbiased but
lost much of its power relative to MMRM. In a reduced
supplementary run with eight visits, a precision-weighted slope
summary closed most of that gap. With eight
visits, MMRM with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom was liberal (Type I
error up to about 0.10) and underestimated its standard error. We argue
that summary statistics should not be framed as generally superior to
MMRM. Prior work (Frison and Pocock; Vossoughi et al.; Chen et al.)
already covers the linear-divergence setting studied here. Instead, the
choice between a rate-of-change estimand and a landmark-visit estimand
should be made first, under the ICH E9(R1) framework, and the estimator
chosen to match it.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} summary measures; ANCOVA; MMRM;
longitudinal clinical trials; estimands; Monte Carlo simulation

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Mixed-model repeated measures (MMRM) has become the default
analytic strategy for longitudinal clinical trials with
continuous endpoints. Regulatory agencies and methodological
guidance documents increasingly endorse MMRM as the primary
analysis\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ichE9R12019}{1}}. The approach has clear theoretical
advantages. It uses all available data under the
missing-at-random (MAR) assumption, it accommodates unbalanced
designs, and it models complex within-subject correlation
structures\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lairdRandomEffectsModels1982}{2}}.

Yet MMRM carries substantial practical burdens. The method
requires the analyst to specify a covariance structure.
Misspecification can inflate Type I error or reduce power\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vossoughiSummaryMeasureAnalysis2012}{3}}. The analyst must also
choose a degrees-of-freedom approximation, such as
Satterthwaite or Kenward-Roger\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-kenwardSmallSampleInference1997}{4},\citeproc{ref-kenwardImprovedApproximation2009}{5}}. With an unstructured
covariance, small samples, and many visits, these choices can
matter. Fitting an unstructured covariance can also be
numerically demanding with small samples and many visits. In
our own simulation, however, the \texttt{mmrm} package\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mmrmPackage2024}{6}} converged in nearly every replicate.
Perhaps most critically, the MMRM treatment effect at a single
visit is not always intuitive to clinical investigators. They
may prefer a single, interpretable summary of each patient's
response trajectory.

An older and somewhat simpler tradition, the summary measures
approach, offers a compelling alternative. It was first
articulated systematically by
Matthews et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-matthewsAnalysisSerialMeasurements1990}{7}}. This
two-stage method first reduces each subject's repeated measurements to a
single scalar summary. Examples include a slope, an area under the
curve, a post-treatment mean, or a change score. The method then applies
standard between-group tests (t-tests, ANCOVA) to these summaries. The
approach does not require the analyst to specify a covariance structure.
With complete, balanced data and normal errors, its t and F tests are
exact. With unequal numbers of visits per subject, the summaries have
unequal variances, and the reference distribution is only approximate.
The approach also produces treatment effect estimates that are directly
interpretable.

We ask a simple question. Are there clinical trial settings in
which inference based on summary statistics is as effective as
MMRM, or better? If so, what do those settings look like?

\subsection{The summary measures tradition}\label{the-summary-measures-tradition}

The intellectual history of summary measures in clinical trials
spans three decades. We begin with
Matthews et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-matthewsAnalysisSerialMeasurements1990}{7}}, who introduced the
two-stage framework in the BMJ. They argued that the
then-common practice of testing group differences at each time
point was flawed in two ways. It was statistically invalid,
owing to multiplicity, and it was clinically uninformative.
They proposed that investigators first identify a summary that
captures the clinically relevant feature of each patient's
trajectory. The investigators would then analyze these
summaries with simple methods. The approach is statistically
valid because there is one summary per subject. As a result,
the summaries are independent across subjects.

Frison and Pocock\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-frisonRepeatedMeasuresClinical1992}{8}} extended this
framework substantially. They considered data that can be summarized by
pre-treatment and post-treatment means. For such data, they showed that
analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) of the post-treatment means is the
method of choice. The ANCOVA adjusts for the pre-treatment means. They
quantified the superiority of ANCOVA over analysis of post-treatment
means alone and over analysis of change scores. ANCOVA dominates both
alternatives in terms of reduced variance and avoidance of bias. We note
that more than one pre-treatment measurement gives meaningful gains in
precision. This is because the pre-treatment mean estimates the true
baseline better than a single observation does.

Frison and Pocock\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-frisonLinearlyDivergentTreatment1997}{9}} addressed the
case where treatment effects diverge linearly over time. For this
setting, the natural summary statistic is each subject's regression
slope. The authors showed that adjusting slopes for baseline values, or
better, for estimated intercepts, yields substantial efficiency gains.
They derived the optimal linear summary statistic for any repeated
measures design. The derivation assumes a known covariance structure and
a known shape for the mean treatment difference. It shows that the
summary measures approach can be made nearly fully efficient.

Senn et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennRepeatedMeasuresClinical2000}{10}} provided a practical
synthesis. They argued that much repeated measures analysis
using mixed models could be replaced by a summary measures
approach with negligible loss of efficiency. Senn and
colleagues described strategies for baseline adjustment when
there are multiple baselines. They also proposed a compromise
trend and mean measure (regression through the origin). This
measure is useful when both the level and the rate of change
matter.

\subsection{Extensions and refinements}\label{extensions-and-refinements}

Dawson\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-dawsonStratificationSummaryStatistic1994}{11}} recognized that
summary statistic distributions depend on the pattern of
missingness. Dawson proposed stratifying summary statistic tests
by missing data pattern. The simulations showed that
stratification increases power and improves robustness to
violations of missing data assumptions.
Dawson\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-dawsonSampleSizeCalculations1998}{12}} later provided a unified
sample size framework for summary statistics. It covers
slopes, post-baseline means, change scores, and final
observations. It also includes modifications for missing data
from staggered entry and random dropouts.

Weinberg and Lagakos\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-weinbergEfficiencyComparisonsRank2001}{13}} examined
the efficiency of rank and permutation tests based on summary statistics
under more complex distributional assumptions, including censored
laboratory markers. Their work showed that the choice of summary
statistic can substantially affect asymptotic relative efficiency. This
is particularly true when the repeated measures show nonlinear growth or
left censoring.

Senn\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-sennChangeBaselineAnalysis2006}{14}} revisited the choice between
ANCOVA and change-score analysis. In doing so, he clarified a
persistent confusion in the literature. Senn argued that
ANCOVA is generally preferable for randomized trials. It
adjusts for chance imbalances in baseline values. It does so
without introducing the bias that afflicts change-score
analysis in non-randomized settings.

\subsection{MMRM: strengths and practical limitations}\label{mmrm-strengths-and-practical-limitations}

The theoretical foundation for MMRM rests on the
random-effects model of Laird and Ware\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lairdRandomEffectsModels1982}{2}}. Laird
and Ware describe their model in two stages, within and
between subjects. That sense of `two-stage' differs from the
summary-measures approach. Their model is fit by a single
likelihood, not by analyzing per-subject summaries. The model
and its extensions are summarized by
Fitzmaurice et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-fitzmauriceAppliedLongitudinalAnalysis2011}{15}}. They provide
maximum likelihood and restricted maximum likelihood (REML)
estimation for models with structured covariance. The
key practical advantage is that MMRM uses all available data.
As a result, it gives valid inference under MAR without
requiring imputation. The DIA/PhRMA working group formalized
MMRM as the recommended primary analysis for continuous
longitudinal endpoints\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodtRecommendationsPrimaryAnalysis2008}{16}}.
Siddiqui et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-siddiquiMMRMLOCFComparison2009}{17}} compared MMRM with
LOCF-ANCOVA across 25 New Drug Application datasets. This work
reinforced the regulatory lineage that led to MMRM's current
default status.

Mallinckrodt et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-mallinckrodtEfficacyDuloxetine2004}{18}} provided an
influential empirical comparison of MMRM and LOCF-ANCOVA across eight
duloxetine trials. MMRM and LOCF agreed on significance in 82\% of 202
comparisons, but MMRM yielded significant results in 12.4\% of cases
where LOCF did not. This work cemented MMRM's position as the preferred
analysis. However, it also revealed that the two approaches diverge
substantively in a non-trivial minority of cases.

MMRM, however, faces several practical limitations that
warrant mention:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Degrees-of-freedom approximations.}
  Kenward and Roger [@kenwardSmallSampleInference1997] proposed a scaled Wald
  statistic with an $F$ approximation that reduces
  small-sample bias. For covariance structures that are
  nonlinear in their parameters, the original adjustment
  depends on the parameterization.
  Kenward and Roger [@kenwardImprovedApproximation2009] traced this to a
  missing term in a Taylor expansion and gave an improved version. The
  \texttt{mmrm} package offers Kenward-Roger inference directly. The
  alternative Satterthwaite approximation is simpler, but it can be
  liberal in small samples. Our own results show this (see Type I
  error).

\item \textbf{Software fragmentation.} SAS \texttt{PROC MIXED}
  has long been the reference implementation. The
  \texttt{mmrm} package [@mmrmPackage2024] was developed to
  provide a comparable R implementation. Its documentation
  reports estimates very close to those from SAS. It also
  reports that the linear Kenward-Roger option matches SAS for
  the unstructured model. We therefore do not treat software
  disagreement as a strong argument against MMRM.

\item \textbf{Covariance structure specification.}
  Vossoughi et al. [@vossoughiSummaryMeasureAnalysis2012] showed that
  misspecifying the error covariance structure in an LMM can
  produce non-sensible results. In their simulations, the
  summary measure approach was not affected in this way. Our
  own simulation does not vary the covariance structure. It
  therefore adds no evidence on this point. In practice,
  analysts often default to unstructured covariance.
  This choice requires estimating $p(p+1)/2$ parameters for
  $p$ time points. That number may exceed the information in
  the data.

\item \textbf{Interpretability.} The MMRM treatment effect at
  a specific visit (for example, week 24) is a difference in
  mean response at one time point. It does not depend on the
  working covariance model, although its estimator's precision
  does. Some investigators find a rate more natural. A slope
  difference answers a direct clinical question: how much
  faster does the treatment group improve per unit time?

\item \textbf{Time-by-covariate interactions.}
  Schuler [@liuMixedModelsCovariateInteractions2021] showed that
  MMRM must include time-by-covariate interactions to achieve
  power gains and robustness against dropout bias relative to
  complete-case ANCOVA. Without these interactions, MMRM may
  not outperform a simple ANCOVA of the final visit.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{When are summary statistics adequate?}\label{when-are-summary-statistics-adequate}

The performance comparison literature provides guidance on
when summary statistics match or exceed MMRM.
Vossoughi et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vossoughiSummaryMeasureAnalysis2012}{3}} conducted
extensive Monte Carlo simulations for linear trend data. They compared
the summary measure approach (SMA), linear mixed models (LMM), and the
unstructured multivariate approach (UMA). They found that SMA completely
dominated UMA. SMA also performed \texttt{convincingly\ close\textquotesingle{}\textquotesingle{}\ to\ the\ best-fitting\ LMM\ in\ testing\ all\ effects.\ Critically,\ when\ the\ LMM\ covariance\ structure\ was\ misspecified,\ the\ LMM\ produced\ unreliable\ results.\ SMA,\ on\ the\ other\ hand,\ remained\ robust.\ They\ concluded\ that\ SMA\ is}a simple, safe and powerful approach in which the loss of
efficiency compared to the best-fitting LMM was generally negligible'\,`.
They also recommended it as ``the first choice to reliably analyse the
linear trend data with a moderate to large number of measurements and/or
small to moderate sample sizes.'\,'

In the Alzheimer's disease trial literature,
Donohue and Aisen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ardMixedModelRepeated2011}{19}} compared MMRM with
continuous-time mixed models in data from five Alzheimer's Disease
Cooperative Study trials. They noted that the categorical-time MMRM
makes no assumption about the shape of the mean trajectory. However, it
may be over-parameterized and less efficient than a linear-trend model.
Chen et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ardSimulationStudyComparing2015}{20}} followed with a
simulation study based on ADAS-Cog data. Both methods had similar Type I
error. The slope model was more powerful in most scenarios. It was
unbiased when the true trajectory was close to linear. MMRM gave
relatively unbiased estimates more generally. The authors therefore
judged an MMRM with relaxed assumptions about progression to be
generally preferable. The slope model may be more powerful when the
trajectory is close to linear.

Based on the literature and on our own simulation, summary
statistics appear to be as effective as MMRM in the following
settings. The list is our
judgment, not a result taken from any one source.

\begin{itemize}
\item Treatment effects are expected to diverge linearly or
  to reach a stable plateau (constant treatment effect after
  an initial period).
\item The number of repeated measurements is moderate to
  large (enabling stable estimation of per-subject summaries).
\item Missing data are rare, or are intermittent and unrelated
  to outcome. Under monotone dropout, an unweighted slope
  summary lost much of its power in our simulation (see
  Results). Weighting the slopes by their precision, or
  stratifying by dropout pattern
  [@dawsonStratificationSummaryStatistic1994], is then advisable.
\item Sample sizes are small to moderate, where MMRM's
  degrees-of-freedom approximations are least reliable.
\item Multiple pre-treatment measurements are available,
  enabling precise baseline adjustment.
\item The scientific question concerns the overall rate of
  change or cumulative treatment benefit, rather than the
  effect at a single specific visit.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Present study}\label{present-study}

Our objectives are threefold. We aim, first, to synthesize the
theoretical and empirical literature comparing summary
statistic and MMRM approaches. Second, we aim to identify
specific clinical trial design features that favor summary
statistics. Third, we conduct a simulation study. It compares
the operating characteristics of summary statistic-based
ANCOVA and MMRM across a range of clinically relevant design
parameters.

The simulation reported here is confined to the linear
random-slope generating model. Under this model, the
near-efficiency of adjusted slope summaries relative to MMRM
is already established\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vossoughiSummaryMeasureAnalysis2012}{3},\citeproc{ref-frisonLinearlyDivergentTreatment1997}{9},\citeproc{ref-ardSimulationStudyComparing2015}{20}}. We therefore do not offer
it as a novel head-to-head methods comparison. Prior
simulation work already covers that ground. The simulation
also does not vary covariance misspecification, nonlinear
trajectories, or MNAR dropout (see Future research). As a
result, it cannot test the robustness and interpretability
arguments made above for summary statistics. Those arguments
rest on the cited literature alone.

The simulation has three more modest aims. First, it checks
that the operating characteristics behave as theory predicts
once each method is scored against its own estimand. Second,
it examines what happens to each method under monotone
dropout. This turns out to be the most informative part of the
study. Third, a reduced supplementary analysis adds a
precision-weighted slope summary and a limited check of
Kenward-Roger inference for MMRM. These are fitted to the same
simulated data sets. A conventional categorical-visit MMRM is
implemented but has not yet been run.

\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{ADEMP structure}\label{ademp-structure}

We report the simulation under the ADEMP framework, following
Morris, White, and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019using}{21}}.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Aims.} Quantify bias, precision, coverage, power, and Type I
  error of summary-statistic-based ANCOVA procedures (slope and
  change-score variants) relative to MMRM under the design
  conditions of interest.
\item
  \textbf{Data-generating mechanism.} Linear mixed model with random
  intercepts and slopes; details in the next subsection.
\item
  \textbf{Estimands.} The three methods target three different
  functionals of the same generating model. We therefore score
  each method against its own true value, not a single shared
  quantity. SMA-slope estimates the treatment
  effect on the per-unit-time slope, \(\delta\). MMRM is fit
  with time entered continuously in the fixed-effects formula
  (\texttt{trt\ *\ time}). The categorical \texttt{visit} factor appears
  alongside it, as the grouping for the unstructured
  covariance. The MMRM \texttt{trt:time} coefficient therefore
  targets the same slope estimand, \(\delta\). This makes it
  directly comparable to SMA-slope. The supplementary
  categorical-visit MMRM targets the final-visit difference,
  \(\delta p\). SMA-change, by
  contrast, estimates the difference in mean post-baseline
  change from baseline. Under linear divergence this equals
  \(\delta \cdot (p + 1) / 2\), not \(\delta\) itself. That is,
  it is the average of \(\delta t\) over \(t = 1, \dots, p\). These
  true values hold for complete data. Under dropout, the
  per-subject summaries average over fewer and earlier visits.
  The SMA-change target then drifts, as the Results show.
\item
  \textbf{Intercurrent events.} The simulation has no intercurrent
  events other than dropout. Dropout is treated as missing
  data under a hypothetical strategy. That is, each method
  targets the effect that would be seen if all subjects stayed
  on treatment and were observed. MMRM is valid for this target
  under MAR. The summary methods are guaranteed to be valid for
  it only when missingness is unrelated to outcome.
\item
  \textbf{Methods.} SMA-slope, SMA-change, and MMRM (see Design
  specifications). A supplementary analysis adds two more, and a
  third is implemented but not yet run.
\item
  \textbf{Performance measures.} Bias, empirical SE, mean model-based
  SE, MSE, coverage of 95\% CIs, and power at \(\alpha = 0.05\).
  The tables report all of these except MSE, which is kept in
  the derived-data object. Power is
  evaluated at the design effect size \(\delta = 0.25\). Type I
  error is evaluated at a null \(\delta = 0\) condition. Each
  measure is reported with its Monte Carlo SE per Morris
  Table 6.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data generating model}\label{data-generating-model}

We consider a two-arm parallel-group randomized trial with \(n\)
subjects per arm. Each subject has one baseline measurement
and \(p\) post-baseline visits at equally spaced intervals. The
response
for subject \(i\) in treatment group \(g\) at time \(t_j\) is
generated as follows.

\[Y_{igj} = \beta_0 + b_{0ig} + (\beta_1 + b_{1ig}) t_j
  + \delta \, g \, t_j + \epsilon_{igj}\]

Here \(g \in \{0, 1\}\) indicates placebo or treatment, and
\(t_j = j\) for \(j = 0, 1, \dots, p\). The intercept is
\(\beta_0\), and \(b_{0ig} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{b0})\) is the
random intercept. Similarly, \(\beta_1\) is the placebo slope
and \(b_{1ig} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{b1})\) is the random slope.
The treatment effect on the slope is \(\delta\). Finally,
\(\epsilon_{igj} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_\epsilon)\) is residual
error. We assume the random effects are independent of the
residual errors. The two random effects are correlated, with
\(\text{Cor}(b_{0ig}, b_{1ig}) = \rho_{01}\). The residuals have
no serial correlation. The true within-subject covariance
therefore has only four parameters. We note that the
unstructured MMRM estimates 10 covariance parameters at
\(p = 4\) and 36 at \(p = 8\). It is heavily over-parameterized
here, which bears on the Type I error results below.

\subsection{Design specifications}\label{design-specifications}

We compare three analytic approaches.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Slope summary + ANCOVA (SMA-slope).}
  For each subject, fit a simple linear regression of
  post-baseline responses on time. The per-subject slope is
  the summary statistic. Compare treatment groups using
  ANCOVA with the baseline measurement as covariate.

\item \textbf{Change score + ANCOVA (SMA-change).}
  For each subject, compute the mean of all post-baseline
  measurements minus the baseline measurement. The primary
  three-way comparison reported here uses ANCOVA with baseline
  adjustment (\texttt{fit\_sma\_change()}). An unadjusted
  two-sample t-test on the same change score is also
  implemented (\texttt{fit\_sma\_change\_ttest()}). However,
  it is not included in the main simulation grid. This keeps a
  baseline-adjusted design common to all three headline
  methods. The t-test variant is available for sensitivity
  analyses.

\item \textbf{MMRM.} We fit a mixed-effects model with fixed
  effects for treatment, time, and baseline as covariate. Time
  is entered continuously, not as a visit factor. As a result,
  the \texttt{trt:time} coefficient is a per-unit-time slope
  effect. It is directly comparable to the SMA-slope estimand.
  The model uses an unstructured covariance over the
  categorical visit factor (\texttt{y \textasciitilde{} trt *
  time + baseline\_y + us(visit | id)}). It also uses
  Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. We note that this is a
  linear-trend mixed model with a correctly specified mean. It
  is not the categorical-visit MMRM with a landmark contrast
  that is usual in trials.
\end{enumerate}

The supplementary analysis fits further methods to the same
simulated data sets. The first is a precision-weighted
slope summary (SMA-slope-W). Each subject's slope is weighted
by the inverse of its estimated variance,
\(1 / (\hat\sigma^2_{b1} + \hat\sigma^2_\epsilon / S_{xx,i})\).
Here \(S_{xx,i}\) is the sum of squared centered visit times for
subject \(i\). The residual variance \(\hat\sigma^2_\epsilon\) is
pooled over all within-subject fits. The slope variance
\(\hat\sigma^2_{b1}\) is a moment estimate, truncated at zero.
The weighted slopes then enter the same ANCOVA. The second is
the continuous-time MMRM above with Kenward-Roger degrees of
freedom (MMRM-KR). The third is the conventional
categorical-visit MMRM (MMRM-visit). It has fixed effects for
treatment by visit and baseline by visit, an unstructured
covariance, and Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom. Its
estimate is the treatment difference at the final visit, whose
true value is \(\delta p\). This third method has been
implemented and unit-tested, but it has not yet been run in the
simulation.

\subsection{Missing data mechanism}\label{missing-data-mechanism}

Missing data are generated under two mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{MCAR}: each post-baseline observation is
  independently missing with probability equal to the
  specified rate. MCAR missingness is therefore intermittent.
\item
  \textbf{MAR}: dropout is monotone. Once missing, a subject
  remains missing for all later visits. The probability of
  dropout at visit \(j\) depends on the observed response at
  visit \(j-1\). Subjects with worse outcomes are more likely to
  drop out. The per-visit hazard is logistic. Its intercept
  starts from the constant hazard that would give the nominal
  rate of dropout by the final visit. The odds are then
  doubled. The dropout logit rises by 0.04 for each unit that
  the previous response falls below 50. This is a weak
  dependence, since the between-subject SD of the response is
  about 5.
\end{itemize}

The nominal rate therefore means different things in the two
arms. Under MCAR it is the per-observation probability of
being missing. Under MAR it roughly matches the fraction of
post-baseline observations missing. However, the fraction
missing at the final visit is much larger, at about twice the
nominal rate. Table \ref{tab:achieved} reports the achieved
rates. The MCAR and MAR arms thus differ in mechanism, in
pattern, and in amount of missing data. Any contrast between
them is confounded. We disclose this design choice rather
than correct it. Separating the three would require new arms
calibrated to a common quantity. A run with a more strongly
informative dropout model is implemented in the supplementary
script but has not yet been run.

Zero-percent missingness is run once per \((n, p)\) cell, not
once per mechanism. This is because MCAR and MAR are identical
when the missingness rate is zero. For each value of \(\delta\),
the design therefore has \(3 \times 2 \times 5 = 30\)
\((n, p, \text{missingness})\) cells. The five missingness
settings are complete data and two rates under each of the two
mechanisms.

\subsection{Simulation procedure}\label{simulation-procedure}

The design has 60 conditions. They cross 3
arm sizes, 2 visit counts, and 5 missingness settings. Each of
these is run at 2 effect sizes, including the null. For each
condition and each of 1000 replications, we record the
point estimate of the treatment effect, its standard error,
and the p-value. We also record whether the 95\% confidence
interval covers the true value for that method (see
Estimands). Monte Carlo SEs follow Table 6 of
Morris et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morris2019using}{21}}.

The script \texttt{analysis/scripts/run\_sim\_09.R} runs the main
simulation. It writes the results, the session information,
and the seed to \texttt{analysis/data/derived\_data/sim\_09.rds}. It also
saves the random number generator state before every
replicate. The supplementary script restores these states. It
therefore regenerates exactly the same data sets and fits the
supplementary methods to them. We checked that the replay
reproduces the stored SMA-slope results to 12 significant
digits. The manuscript only reads the saved results.

\subsection{Performance metrics}\label{performance-metrics}

For each design condition and analytic method, we compute:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bias}: mean estimate minus true treatment effect
\item
  \textbf{Empirical SE}: standard deviation of estimates across
  replications
\item
  \textbf{Mean model-based SE}: mean of estimated standard errors
\item
  \textbf{Power}: proportion of replications with \(p < 0.05\)
\item
  \textbf{Coverage}: proportion of 95\% CIs containing the true
  value
\item
  \textbf{Convergence rate}: proportion of replications where the
  model fit converged (relevant for MMRM only)
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Headline findings}\label{headline-findings}

We begin with complete data at the nonzero design effect
(\(\delta = 0.25\)). Here all three methods recovered their own
estimand, with absolute bias at most
0.040. The estimands
are \(\delta\) for SMA-slope and MMRM, and \(\delta (p+1)/2\) for
SMA-change (see Estimands). With complete data, SMA-slope and
MMRM had nearly the same power. At \(n = 100\) and \(p = 8\), for
example, power was 0.89 for SMA-slope and
0.89 for MMRM. MMRM convergence was at least
0.998 in every condition.

The main result concerns monotone (MAR) dropout. Under it, the
unweighted slope summary lost much of its power, while MMRM
did not. At \(n = 100\) and \(p = 8\), SMA-slope power fell to
0.44 and 0.28
at the nominal 10\% and 20\% MAR rates. MMRM power was
0.82 and 0.75. Under
intermittent MCAR missingness at 20\%, the two methods were
close at this arm size (0.79 and
0.82). With smaller arms the MCAR gap was
larger. At \(n = 30\) and \(p = 8\), for example, power was
0.30 for SMA-slope and 0.39 for MMRM.
However, MMRM is liberal in that cell (see Type I error). The
empirical SE of SMA-slope rose
from 0.091 under MCAR 20\% to
0.195 under MAR 20\%. SMA-slope stayed
nearly unbiased under MAR, with absolute bias at most
0.022. Its coverage
stayed between 0.944 and
0.963. The loss is one of
precision, not of validity.

The mechanism is straightforward. Under monotone dropout, many
subjects have only two or three post-baseline visits. Their
least-squares slopes are far noisier than full-trajectory
slopes. The unweighted second-stage ANCOVA gives them the same
weight as everyone else. Subjects with fewer than two
post-baseline visits are dropped altogether. We note that the
MAR arm also has more missing data than its label suggests
(Table \ref{tab:achieved}). The size of the loss therefore
reflects the dropout pattern and the larger amount of missing
data together. The supplementary analysis below shows that
weighting the slopes by their precision closes most of the
gap to MMRM at \(p = 8\).

SMA-change behaved differently. Under MAR 20\% at \(p = 8\), its
bias was between -0.21 and
-0.16, against a true value of
1.125. This is not estimator
noise. For dropouts, the post-baseline mean averages over fewer
and earlier visits. Its target therefore drifts toward smaller
values. In other words, the SMA-change estimand is not fixed
under dropout unless an intercurrent-event strategy is stated.

\subsection{Type I error}\label{type-i-error}

At the null condition (\(\delta = 0\)), rejection rates at
\(\alpha = 0.05\) estimate Type I error. With 1000
replicates, the Monte Carlo SE of a rate near 0.05 is about
0.007. SMA-slope rejection rates
ranged from
0.031 to
0.061.
SMA-change rates ranged from
0.039 to
0.070.
Of these 60 rates,
2 lay more than 1.96 Monte Carlo SEs below
0.05 and 1 lay that far above. That is
about what chance alone would produce. Both methods are
therefore consistent with nominal size. Neither is systematically
conservative.

MMRM, by contrast, was liberal when there were many visits. At
\(p = 8\) its rejection rate was 0.076 to 0.099 at \(n = 30\) per
arm. It was 0.057 to 0.081 at \(n = 50\), and 0.059 to 0.070 at
\(n = 100\). In 13 of
30 null cells, the MMRM rate
exceeded 0.05 by more than 1.96 Monte Carlo SEs. At \(p = 4\) it
was closer to nominal, at 0.055 to 0.069 for \(n = 30\) and
0.050 to 0.059 and 0.047 to 0.061 for the larger arms.

The cause is visible in the standard errors. At \(p = 8\) and
\(\delta = 0.25\), the mean model-based SE of MMRM was only
81 to
95 percent of its
empirical SE for \(n = 30\) and 50. At \(n = 30\) the ratio was at
most 88
percent. MMRM coverage in these cells was
0.897 to
0.939. This is the known
small-sample behavior of the Satterthwaite approximation. It
is made worse here by fitting 36 unstructured covariance
parameters to data whose true covariance has four.

One consequence matters for the power comparisons. Where MMRM
appears more powerful at \(p = 8\) and small \(n\), the two tests
do not have equal size. Those power differences should not be
read as an efficiency advantage for MMRM. The supplementary
analysis refits MMRM with Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom to
check this explanation.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

Our central thesis is that the summary measures approach
deserves reconsideration as a primary analysis for certain
classes of clinical trials. The theoretical literature begins
with Matthews et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-matthewsAnalysisSerialMeasurements1990}{7}}. It was
formalized by Frison and Pocock\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-frisonRepeatedMeasuresClinical1992}{8},\citeproc{ref-frisonLinearlyDivergentTreatment1997}{9}}. This literature
establishes that summary statistics with ANCOVA baseline
adjustment can achieve near-optimal efficiency. The result
applies to detecting linear treatment effects. The simulation
work of Vossoughi et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-vossoughiSummaryMeasureAnalysis2012}{3}} adds empirical
support. In their study, a misspecified LMM covariance gave
unreliable results, while the summary measure approach did
not. Our own simulation never varies the covariance structure.
It therefore cannot confirm or refute that robustness claim.

Our simulation does add one practical lesson. With complete
data, the unweighted slope summary matched MMRM closely. With
intermittent missingness it lost some power. Under monotone
dropout it lost much more. Its
estimates stayed nearly unbiased and its coverage stayed close
to nominal. However, its power fell well below that of MMRM.
The remedy is already in the summary-measures literature.
Slopes estimated from fewer visits should carry less weight,
or the analysis should be stratified by dropout pattern\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-dawsonStratificationSummaryStatistic1994}{11}}. In our
supplementary analysis with eight visits, a simple
precision-weighted slope summary closed most of the power gap
to MMRM. In our view, an
unweighted slope summary should not be used as a primary
analysis when substantial monotone dropout is expected.

The practical advantages of summary statistics are not merely
aesthetic. Consider multi-regional trials, where statistical
expertise varies across sites. A method that needs no
covariance structure selection has real operational value. So
does a method whose treatment effect is directly
interpretable. However, two common arguments against MMRM
proved weak in our setting. First, the \texttt{mmrm} package
converged in nearly every replicate, even with 36 covariance
parameters and 30 subjects per arm. Second, its documentation
reports close agreement with SAS. The degrees-of-freedom
question, on the other hand, was real. With many visits and
small samples, Satterthwaite inference for an unstructured
MMRM was liberal, and its model-based SE was too small.

Summary statistics are sometimes said to handle missing data
more naturally than repeated measures models. We think this
claim needs care. A slope or mean computed from all available
post-baseline data does use all observed information for that
subject. It needs no model for the missing data mechanism.
However, that is because it implicitly assumes that missingness
is unrelated to outcome. Our SMA-change results
show what happens otherwise. Under dropout its target drifted,
and its bias reached about 14 to 19 percent of the true value.

The Alzheimer's disease trial literature provides an
instructive case. Donohue and Aisen\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ardMixedModelRepeated2011}{19}} noted that a
categorical-time MMRM may be over-parameterized relative to a
linear-trend model. Chen et al.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ardSimulationStudyComparing2015}{20}} found the
slope model more powerful in most of their scenarios, with
similar Type I error. They nevertheless judged an MMRM with
relaxed assumptions about progression to be generally
preferable. More recent work has moved toward flexible models
of the mean trajectory. Examples are natural cubic splines\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-donohueNaturalCubicSplines2023}{22}} and progression models\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raketProgressionModels2022}{23}}. The slope summary still answers
a direct clinical question: `Does the drug slow decline?'
However, it does so reliably only when decline is close to
linear.

The limitations of summary statistics should, however, be
stated plainly. When treatment effects are non-monotone (for
example, an initial benefit that wanes), no single summary
captures the full trajectory. In that setting, MMRM's
flexibility in modeling visit-specific effects is genuinely
advantageous. Under substantial MNAR dropout, the summary
statistic approach may inherit bias. This is because
per-subject summaries computed from truncated trajectories
underrepresent the full response. Finally, the scientific
question may genuinely concern the treatment effect at a
specific landmark visit rather than the overall trajectory.
This is a common design choice in disease-modification trials.
It includes Alzheimer's disease trials with a primary endpoint
at a fixed late visit. In that case, MMRM's ability to
estimate visit-specific contrasts is essential. We note that
no general FDA mandate fixes that visit at a particular week.
The choice of landmark is a protocol-specific design decision.

Schuler\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liuMixedModelsCovariateInteractions2021}{24}} highlighted another
subtlety. MMRM is guaranteed to gain power over complete-case
ANCOVA only when time-by-covariate interactions are included.
Without these, MMRM may be no better than the simpler method it
purports to replace. This finding matters in settings where
analysts may omit these interactions. Related work on baseline
handling is also relevant. Constrained longitudinal data
analysis treats baseline as part of the response\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-liangLongitudinalPrePost2000}{25},\citeproc{ref-luEfficiencyConstrainedLDA2010}{26}}.
It offers a further alternative to baseline-adjusted ANCOVA.

The estimands framework of ICH E9(R1)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ichE9R12019}{1}} provides a
useful lens\textsuperscript{see also \citeproc{ref-mallinckrodtEstimandsEstimators2020}{27}}.
We note first what it does not imply. The MMRM estimand at a
visit is a difference in marginal means at that visit. It is
defined by the population and by the strategy for
intercurrent events. It is not conditional on the working
covariance model. With a saturated mean and an unstructured
covariance, MMRM estimates it consistently under MAR. The
working covariance affects the estimator's efficiency, and
its validity if misspecified. It does not change the estimand.
Conversely, the slope estimand is not free of design choices.
Under dropout, a per-subject slope depends on which visits
each subject contributes. A slope analysis therefore needs an
explicit intercurrent-event strategy, just as MMRM does. The
real distinction is between estimands, not between more and
less model-dependent ones. A difference in mean rates of change
and a difference at a landmark visit answer different clinical
questions. The choice between them should come first. The
choice of estimator should follow.

\section{Future research}\label{future-research}

Several directions, in our view, merit further investigation.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Nonlinear summary statistics.} The present review
  focuses on linear summaries (slopes, means, change scores).
  Nonlinear summaries may capture treatment effects that
  linear summaries miss. Examples include time to a
  clinically meaningful threshold and the area under the
  response-time curve (AUC). Efficiency comparisons under realistic
  data-generating models would be valuable.
\item
  \textbf{Robust variance estimation.} Sandwich (HC) standard
  errors for ANCOVA in randomized trials are well studied\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-wangAnalysisCovarianceRandomized2019}{28}}. Their behavior
  with slope summaries of unequal precision, as under
  dropout, has received less attention. A comparison with
  the precision-weighted summary used here would be useful.
\item
  \textbf{Multiple imputation with summary statistics.} When
  missing data rates are substantial, summary statistics
  could be computed within each multiply imputed dataset and
  pooled via Rubin's rules. This could extend the approach
  to settings where simple complete-case analysis is
  inadequate.
\item
  \textbf{Software tools.} A dedicated R package would lower the
  barrier to adoption. It would implement the summary
  measures approach with ANCOVA, baseline adjustment, sample
  size formulas, and diagnostic plots.
\item
  \textbf{Head-to-head regulatory submissions.} Empirical
  evidence from regulatory submissions would illuminate the
  practical concordance of the two approaches. The relevant
  submissions are those that presented both MMRM and summary
  statistics, as co-primary or sensitivity analyses.
\item
  \textbf{Bayesian summary statistics.} Integrating the summary
  measures approach with Bayesian sample size determination\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-baghfalakiBayesianSampleSize2019}{29}} and posterior inference
  could provide a unified framework for design and analysis.
\item
  \textbf{Extension to non-Gaussian endpoints.} The summary
  measures framework is well developed for continuous
  outcomes. Extension to binary or count outcomes (for
  example, proportion of visits with response, total adverse
  events) remains underexplored.
\end{enumerate}
